\title{End-behavior of a Kaplan-Meier Estimator for Repeated Events}
\author{Jacob Spear}

\begin{abstract}
Assuming a mixture cure model of customer return, we show that the Kaplan-Meier estimator applied to inter-visit
gap times converges to the proportion of censored visits, regardless of the underlying rates of attrition.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

We want to model the following scenario.  A restaurant tracks its customers' patterns of repeated visits.
Data collection begins on day $d=1$.  On each day between day $1$ and day $x$, the final day of data collection,
some constant number, $k$, of new customers discover the restaurant and make their first visit.  Thus, on day $x$,
$kx$ customers in total have visited the restaurant.  To determine time to return, we will make independent and 
identically distributed draws from some probability distribution on the \textbf{positive} integers with finite moments.  It will be convenient for the number of these draws to be deterministic, depending only on $x$ and not on the results
of these draws.  For each customer $i$, we make one draw $T_{ij}$ for each positive integer $j$, representing the time 
to return \textit{if the customer visits on day $j$}.  

Note that if customer $1$ has $T_{1,1}=3$, then their next visit will be on day $4$, and so $T_{1,2}$ and $T_{1,3}$
have no influence on their behavior at all.  

Customer $i$ visits the restaurant for the first time on day $d_i$.  For each customer, we randomly select 
a day of attrition $D_i$, on the interval $\left[d_i,\infty\right)$ such that the CDF $f_D$ of the distribution from which 
the $D_i$ are drawn satisfies:
$y_1>y_2 \implies f_{D}(y_1|T_{ij}) \leq f_{D}(y_2|T_{ij})$.
We do not assume that the $D_i$ are independent of the $T_{ij}$.  

The above model approximates a \textit{mixture cure} model, since the ``cured'' population (who will never return to the restaurant)
are modeled separately from the \textit{at-risk} population who may still return. 

\section{The KM Estimator}

The Kaplan-Meier estimator is the standard approach in oncology (and other medical disciplines) to estimating the time
to some event (often called \textit{survival time}) in the presence of censored data.  The inputs to the estimator are times
$T_i$ and event/censoring indicators $\delta_i$.  The standard approach assumes one row per subject, with
the $\delta_i$ indicating whether the event has occurred ($\delta_i=1$) or not ($\delta_i=0$) for each subject.  Typically
the estimator is written as a product of the proportion of subjects \textit{at risk} at a given time who \textit{survive}, or 
do not have the event.  However, we can factor these terms as ratios $\frac{m-1}{m}$ by treating each event time 
as infinitesimally distinct.  Suppose we have $n$ records.  Ordering the $T_i$ in increasing order, 

\[S(t)=\prod_{\{i:T_i\le t\}} \left(\frac{n-(i+1)}{n-i}\right)^{\delta_i}.\]

Suppose we take a snapshot of our data on day $d$.  Keep in mind, the restaurant wouldn't be able to observe
each of the $T_{ij}$ - they would only observe the visits that actually occurred.

For each customer, let $c_i$ be the time from their last visit to day $d$ - the censoring time.  Note that
$c_i$ is a function of $x$, of the $T_{ij}$, and of the $D_i$.  If the customer discontinued at time $D_i$,
their $c_i$ would be the time from their last visit before time $D_i$. 

On day $x$, we wish to estimate time-to-return.  One plausible approach is to use the Kaplan-Meier
estimator and to treat each visit as a separate observation.  The final visits for each customer are the censored
visits, and we have no deterministic way to distinguish among these censored visits which are due to true attrition
and which correspond to a customer who will return but hasn't yet.

For each visit, all we know is the time to the next visit (if it occurred), and the censoring time if it 
did not.  We also know whether each visit is censored.  Each non-censored visit time is one of the $T_{ij}$,
and the $c_i$ are drawn from some distribution on $\left[d_i,x\right)$ which depends on the $T_{ij}$.  

To simplify the analysis, we assume that we have chosen the distribution of the $D_i$ so that the $c_i$
are asymptotically uniform on $\left[0,x\right]$.

We re-index the non-censored visits in order of non-decreasing length as $\widehat(T)_\ell$
and we re-index the censoring time $\widehat{c}_i$ similarly. 

We interleave the $\widehat(T)_\ell$ and $\widehat{c}_i$ with the standard convention that, in the case of ties,
$\widehat{c}$ (the censored time) occurs after $\widehat(T)$ (the event time).

Call these times $\tau_i$ and let $\delta_i=1$ if $\tau_i$ corresponds to an event $\widehat{T}_\ell$ 
or a censoring time $\widehat{c}$.  We are finally ready to write down the formula for the KM estimator.
Recall that $kx$ is the total number of customers.  Let $V_x$ be the total number of visits 
which have occurred by time $x$.  Note that $V_x$ is bounded above by $kx^2$.  Then


\[S_x(t) = \prod_{\left\{i : \tau_i = t\right\}}{\left(\frac{V_x-(i+1)}{V_x-i}\right)^{\delta_i}}.\]

Really, we are only interested in the final value $S_x(x)$, which we abbreviate as $S_x$:

\[S_x = \prod_{i=1}^{V_x} \left(\frac{V_x-(i+1)}{V_x-i}\right)^{\delta_i}.\]

Our main result is:

\begin{theorem}
    With $\widehat{c}_i$ uniformly distributed in $\left[0,x\right]$, $\widehat{T}_{\ell}$ i.i.d. as above,
    and $\tau_i$ defined as above, let $V_x=V_e + V_c$, where $V_e$ is the number of $\widehat{T}_{\ell}$ and $V_c$
    the number of $\widehat{c}_i$.  Then 
\[\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} S_x \cdot \frac{V_x}{V_c} \] converges in probability to $1$.
\end{theorem}

\section{Bounds for the asymptote of the KM estimator}

Note that $S_x \le \frac{V_c}{V_x}$, because if all events occur before all censored records, the estimator simply becomes:

\[\frac{V_x-1}{V_x} \cdot \frac{V_x-2}{V_x-1}\dots \frac{V_x-V_e}{V_x-V_e+1} = \frac{V_c}{V_x}.\]

and that any other value of $S_x$ would replace some of the terms of this product with smaller values.

Thus, we wish to find an asyptototic lower bound for $S_x$ (in probability) which converges
to $\frac{V_c}{V_x}$ in the limit (i.e., the quotient of the two converges to $1$ in probability).  
Note that by our assumptions of how the customers originate ($k$ per day), we have
$V_c=kx$ and we have $V_x < kx^2$.

We will argue that relatively few of the censored times occur before the final uncensored time.  
Let $b_x$ be the number of such censored times.

We will construct a function $\varphi(x)$ such that $\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}P(b_x < \varphi(x))=1$  and such that
$\varphi(x)/x$ approaches $0$ in the limit.  Assuming that $\varphi$ exists, we can prove the theorem:

\begin{proof}
    As above,
    \[S_x = \prod_{i=1}^{V_x} \left(\frac{V_x-(i+1)}{V_x-i}\right)^{\delta_i}.\]
    we imagine rearranging the $\delta_i$ so that the first $b_x$ terms are $1$.  This replaces some
    terms of the product with smaller values.  Therefore,
    \[S_x \ge \frac{V_x-b_x-1}{V_x-b_x}\dots \frac{V_x-V_e-b_x-1}{V_x-V_e-b_x} = \frac{V_c-b_x-1}{V_x-b_x}.\]

    Therefore:

    \begin{align}
        \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}S_x\frac{V_x}{V_c} &\geq \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} \frac{V_c-b_x-1}{V_x-b_x} \cdot \frac{V_c}{V_x} \\
        &\geq \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\frac{V_c-b_x-1}{V_c} 
    \end{align}

    Considering probabilities,
    \begin{align}    
        \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}P\left(S_x\frac{V_x}{V_c}<1\right) &\leq  \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}P\left(\frac{V_c-b_x-1}{V_c}<1\right) \\
        &\leq \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}P(b_x < \varphi(x))\cdot P\left(\frac{V_c-\varphi(x)-1}{V_c}<1\right) +  \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}P(b_x > \varphi(x)) \\
        &\leq \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}P\left(\frac{V_c-\varphi(x)-1}{V_c}<1\right) \\
        &= \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}P\left(\frac{kx-\varphi(x)-1}{kx}<1\right)  \\
        &= \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} P\left(\frac{\varphi(x)}{kx}>0\right)= 0
    \end{align}
    as desired. \qed
\end{proof}

Thus, all that remains is to choose an appropriate $\varphi(x)$.  For the above proof to hold, we need
$\varphi(x) < o(x)$.  To get $b_x < \varphi(x)$ with probability $1$, we will need $\varphi(x) > o(\sqrt{x})$. 
Any such function will do.  It remains to show that this lower bound on $\varphi(x)$ is sufficient to get the
bound on $b_x$ as $x$ approaches infinity.

Recall that $b_x$ is the number of $\widehat{c}_i$ which occur before the maximum of the
$\widehat{T}_\ell$.  However the number of $\widehat{T}_\ell$ depends on the $\widehat{T}_\ell$ and $\widehat{c}_i$
in a complicated manner.  To simplify the analysis, we instead consider $\star{b}_x$, the number of $\widehat{c}_i$
less than or equal to the maximum of the precisely $kx^2$ i.i.d $T_{ij}$.  We drop the double index on the $T$ as we no longer
need to keep track of the customer to which each interval belongs.  

We use the following fact (verifiable with straightforward calculus) without proof.  

\begin{fact}
    \label{fact}
    If $k>1$ and $x\in \left[1,\infty\right)$, then
    \[\left(1-\frac{1}{kx^4}\right)^{kx^2} > 1 - \frac{1}{x}.\]
\end{fact}


We'll need two lemmas:
\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:Tmax}
    \[\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} \sqrt{x}P\left(\max(T_j)>\sqrt[4]{x}\right)=0.\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Note that the maximum is taken over $kx^2$ i.i.d. $T_j$.  For any $T$ with this distribution, recall that $T$ has finite $m$th moment for all $m$, so we have:
\[\lim_{t\rightarrow \infty} t^mP\left(T>t\right)=0.\]

We need the particular case where $t=\sqrt[4]{x}$ and $m=16$.  Multiplying by the constant $k$, we have:

\[\lim_{t\rightarrow \infty} kx^4P\left(T>\sqrt[4]{x}\right)=0.\]

In particular, for $x$ sufficiently large,

\[kx^4P\left(T>\sqrt[4]{x}\right) < 1.\]

or, suggestively, by Fact~\ref{fact} above:

\[P\left(T>\sqrt[4]{x}\right) < \frac{1}{kx^4}.\]

Applying this to the fact, we shrink $\frac{1}{kx^4}$ to $P\left(T>\sqrt[4]{x}\right)$, increasing the LHS of the inequality, so

\[\left(1-P\left(T>\sqrt[4]{x}\right)\right)^{kx^2} > 1 - \frac{1}{x}.\]

Rearranging, for $x$ \textit{sufficiently large}, we have what we'll eventually need:

\begin{equation}
    \label{keyineq}
1-\left(1-P\left(T>\sqrt[4]{x}\right)\right)^{kx^2} < \frac{1}{x}.
\end{equation}

Because the $T_j$ are independent, we have

\[ P\left(\max(T_j)>\sqrt[4]{x}\right) = 1 - \left(P\left(T_j \le \sqrt[4]{x}\right)\right)^{kx^2}. \]

For simplicity, we drop the subscript and refer to a random variable $T$ with the same distribution as the $T_j$.  Then,

\[\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} \sqrt{x}P\left(\max(T_j)>\sqrt[4]{x}\right) =\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\sqrt{x}\left( 1 -  \left(1 -P\left(T > \sqrt[4]{x}\right)\right)^{kx^2} \right).\]

But by equation \ref{keyineq}, we now have (for $x$ sufficiently large):

\[\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} \sqrt{x}P\left(\max(T_j)>\sqrt[4]{x}\right) < \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\sqrt{x} \cdot \frac{1}{x} =0.\]

as desired. \qed
\end{proof}

With this result, we no longer need to distinguish the $T_j$, we only care about their maximum, which we denote with the random
variable $T_{\max}$.

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:pi}
$\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} \sqrt{x} \cdot P\left(c_i < T_{\max}\right) = 0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Note that by our assumption that the $c_i$ are uniformly distributed (this is the only place we use this assumption,
and it is easy to see how we might weaken it), for any $N$,
\[P\left(c_i < N\right) = \frac{N}{X}.\] 
We will bound $\sqrt{x} \cdot P\left(c_i < T_{\max}\right)$ above.  Decompose $P\left(c_i < T_{\max}\right)$ conditionally on $T_{\max}$ at the boundary
$\sqrt[4]{x}$.
\begin{align}
P\left(c_i < T_{\max}\right) &= P\left(T_{\max} \le \sqrt[4]{x}\right)\cdot P\left(c_i < T_{\max} \mid T_{\max} \le \sqrt[4]{x}\right) \\
&\quad + P\left(T_{\max} > \sqrt[4]{x}\right)\cdot P\left(c_i < T_{\max} \mid T_{\max} > \sqrt[4]{x}\right) \\
&\le  P\left(T_{\max} \le \sqrt[4]{x}\right)\cdot P\left(c_i < \sqrt[4]{x}\right) + P\left(T_{\max} > \sqrt[4]{x}\right) \\
&= P\left(T_{\max} \le \sqrt[4]{x}\right)\cdot \frac{\sqrt[4]{x}}{x} + P\left(T_{\max} > \sqrt[4]{x}\right) \\
&\le \frac{\sqrt[4]{x}}{x} + P\left(T_{\max} > \sqrt[4]{x}\right) 
\end{align}.
Now, we take the limit as $x$ approaches infinity and apply Lemma~\ref{lem:Tmax}.
\begin{align}
\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} \sqrt{x} \cdot P\left(c_i < T_{\max}\right) &\le \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} \sqrt{x} 
\cdot \left(\frac{\sqrt[4]{x}}{x} + P\left(T_{\max} > \sqrt[4]{x}\right)\right) \\
&= \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{x}} + \sqrt{x}P\left(T_{\max} > \sqrt[4]{x}\right) \\
&= 0
\end{align}.
As desired, $\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} \sqrt{x} \cdot P\left(c_i < T_{\max}\right) = 0$. \qed
\end{proof}

Finally, we're ready to prove our result. 

\begin{proposition}
    Suppose $\varphi(x) > o(\sqrt{x})$.  Then 
    \[\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty } P\left(b_x < \varphi(x)\right) =1.\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Let $\pi(x) = P\left(c_i < T_{\max}\right)$.  Then by Lemma~\ref{lem:pi}, $\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\sqrt{x}\pi(x) = 0$.
    Moreover, note that $b_x$ is the sum of $kx$ Bernoulli-distributed variables with $p=\pi(x)$, and so it has distribution 
    $\text{Binom}(kx,\pi(x))$.  By Markov's inequality:
    \[P\left(b_x \ge \varphi(x)\right) \le \frac{kx\pi(x)}{\varphi(x)}.\]

    Hence,
    \[P\left(b_x < \varphi(x)\right) \ge 1 - \frac{kx\pi(x)}{\varphi(x)}.\]

    Finally, taking the limit:

    \begin{align}
        \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} P\left(b_x < \varphi(x)\right) &\ge 1 - \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} \frac{kx\pi(x)}{\varphi(x)} \\
        &\ge 1 - \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} k\sqrt{x}\pi(x)\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\varphi(x)}
    \end{align}.
    By Lemma~\ref{lem:pi} and because $\varphi(x) > o(\sqrt{x})$, this is equal to $1$. \qed
\end{proof}
